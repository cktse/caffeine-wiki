# Processing Terminology

## Why This Page Exists

Processing terminology in coffee evolved ad hoc, without industry standardization. The same words mean different things depending on the country of origin, the era, and who is using them. When sourcing green coffee, a lot described as "anaerobic natural" from one producer and "anaerobic natural" from another may represent meaningfully different processes.

This page documents the known ambiguities and — where possible — clarifies what each term actually refers to.

## Ambiguous Terms and Their Problems

### "Natural" / "Dry Process" / "Sun-Dried"

All three are used for the same thing: drying coffee with the cherry intact (no depulping before drying). Problems:

- **"Natural"** — most common term, but FDA defines "natural food" as "minimum of processing or treatment with preservatives," making the word almost meaningless for technical communication
- **"Sun-dried"** — too inclusive; many other processing styles (washed, honey) also dry in the sun
- **"Dry process"** — accurate (no water used), but can be confused with "dry milling" — the export preparation step involving hulling parchment, density sorting, and bagging

**Kornman's recommended alternative**: "dried in the cherry" or "dried in the fruit" — unambiguous, process-descriptive.

### "Washed" vs. "Wet Process"

These are not synonymous:
- **"Washed"**: coffee that is depulped, fermented, washed, and dried — the standard clean-cup process
- **"Wet process"**: refers to the use of water in processing. Includes washed AND honey/pulped natural (which uses water during pulping but skips the washing channel)

**Practical implication**: "wet processed" on a bag does not guarantee "washed." When sourcing, ask specifically whether the coffee went through a washing channel after fermentation.

### "Honey" / "Pulped Natural" / "Semi-Washed"

Three names for essentially the same process: depulped, then dried with mucilage intact (no fermentation tank, no washing channel). Origin of terms:
- **"Pulped natural"**: English term, Brazil origin; emerged early 1990s; emphasizes that the skin is removed but it dries like a natural
- **"Cereza descascado"**: Portuguese original = "de-husked cherry"
- **"Semi-lavado"**: Spanish = "semi-washed"; used in Latin America; small variation (more water use during pulping)
- **"Fully washed"**: historically used to distinguish washed from semi-washed; now rarely needed

Today "honey" has largely won in English-language specialty commerce. The color labels (black/red/yellow/white) vaguely indicate mucilage level, from most to least — but **these are not standardized across producers or countries**. See [[shared/honey-processing]] for the color label system and its caveats.

**Brazilian usage — "semidry / CD":** In Brazil this same family is called **semidry**, *natural pulped*, *pulped cherry*, or **CD (cereja descascado**, "de-husked cherry"). It is the **dominant** specialty method in Brazil (wet is only ~20%), chosen because Brazil's mixed-maturity harvest is mechanically depulped to drop unripe fruit before drying — yielding a fuller body than washed while avoiding immature-bean astringency. Sera et al. 2025 note Brazilian producers reported **23 different terms** for these postharvest stages, a concrete illustration of the non-standardization above. (source: [[varietals/sources/brazil-specialty-coffee-sera-2025]])

### "Anaerobic" — The Biggest Terminological Problem

Used increasingly to describe any sealed low-oxygen fermentation. The fundamental problem: *all fermentation is technically anaerobic* — the scientific definition of fermentation is metabolic energy release without oxygen. Applying "anaerobic" as a processing category creates a meaningless distinction.

In practice, "anaerobic" in specialty coffee commerce usually means:
- Coffee held in a sealed container (with one-way airlock) for some period of fermentation
- Can refer to whole cherry (natural) OR depulped parchment (washed-adjacent) OR various in-between states
- CO₂ may be self-generated (SIAF) or externally injected — both may be called "anaerobic"

**Kornman's recommendation**: use **"sealed-tank fermentation"** or **"sealed-tank maceration"** as more precise alternatives. Supplement with specifics: was the coffee depulped? Submerged in water? How long?

### "Carbonic Maceration"

Wine industry term with a specific meaning (CO₂ triggers intracellular fermentation within the intact grape berry). In coffee commerce, **almost exclusively refers to whole cherry added to a sealed tank** — the intracellular mechanism is not confirmed to work the same way in coffee (different cherry anatomy). The sensory results (aromatic, fruity, wine-like) have been documented, but the mechanism is not directly equivalent to wine carbonic maceration.

**Practical use**: when a producer says "carbonic maceration," assume whole cherry in a sealed tank with some CO₂ environment. Ask whether CO₂ was externally added or self-generated.

### "Lactic" / "Lactic Fermentation"

A term for sealed-tank processes where lactic acid bacteria dominate. LAB proliferate in anaerobic environments as pH drops, producing lactic acid and various flavor compounds. Not a distinct processing method — rather, a description of the dominant microbiology during sealed-tank fermentation. Sometimes used as a marketing label for SIAF or anaerobic washed coffees.

### "Double Washed" / "Triple Washed"

Precision terms used primarily in Kenya and Ethiopia context:
- **Double washed**: standard fermentation + post-fermentation soak in clean water before drying
- **Triple washed**: pre-fermentation cherry flotation + fermentation + post-fermentation soak

The post-fermentation soak is believed to remove additional surface compounds and contribute to the clarity and brightness of Kenyan and Ethiopian washed coffees. Rarely indicated on bags from other origins even when practiced.

### "Fully Washed" / "Semi-Washed"

Historical distinction: "fully washed" = went through washing channel after fermentation; "semi-washed" = skipped washing (i.e., honey/pulped natural). "Semi-washed" has largely fallen out of English-language specialty commerce in favor of "honey."

## Kornman's Processing Flowchart

It is the primary step-by-step decomposition of processing methods in this wiki, and directly operationalizes the terminology critique above: the ambiguous ("Sometimes") cells are exactly where producer descriptions diverge.

**What the table clarifies:**

- **Washed vs. Honey** is defined at the *drying* row, not depulping: both depulp, but washed dries in *clean* parchment (mucilage removed via ferment + wash) while honey dries in parchment *with mucilage*.
- **Triple vs. Double washed**: triple adds cherry flotation ("Always") on top of the double's post-fermentation soak; double's flotation is only "Sometimes."
- **Eco-pulped / demucilaged** mimics washed output (dries in clean parchment, "Always") while skipping traditional fermentation and washing channels ("Rarely") — the mechanical reason these lots get labeled "washed" despite not being fermented or washed.
- **Wet hulled** is the only method with "Dried as Seed / green bean = Always" and merely "Partially" dried in clean parchment — encoding the wet-parchment-removal step that defines Giling Basah (see [[shared/wet-hulling]]).
- **Anaerobic vs. Carbonic**: both are "sealed-tank Always," but carbonic is specifically *whole cherry*; anaerobic may be depulped ("Sometimes"). Carbonic is effectively a whole-cherry subset of sealed-tank.

## Sourcing Implications

When evaluating a green lot described with processing terminology:

1. **Ask what equipment was used** (hand pulper? ecopulper? sealed tanks?)
2. **Ask the state of the coffee** at each stage (whole cherry? depulped? submerged in water?)
3. **Ask the timeline** (how many hours/days in tank? how many days drying?)
4. **Don't assume "anaerobic" means CO₂ was added** — it may be SIAF (self-induced, no external gas)
5. **Don't assume honey color labels are standardized** — a "red honey" from Costa Rica is not the same process as a "red honey" from Ethiopia

"Natural process" says little. "Whole cherry dried on raised beds for 21 days, turned 4x daily, altitude 1,800m, pre-sorted for ripeness" says a lot.
