# The Mechanism Behind Astringency in Coffee

## Summary

Jonathan Gagné proposes that astringency in coffee is primarily a **filtration problem**, not a flow-evenness or over-extraction problem. Astringent compounds in coffee are nearly insoluble at typical brew temperatures — they float as suspended particles in the slurry and must be physically filtered out by the coffee bed. Grinding finer liberates more undissolved astringent compounds by breaking more coffee cells, creating a grind-size ceiling beyond which even perfect filtration fails. The specific compound responsible was communicated to Gagné by Samo Smrke (food scientist, ZHAW) but withheld pending peer-reviewed publication at time of writing (August 2022). The model was empirically tested via a 70°C brew experiment (EY 27%, no astringency, but significantly dull cup profile).

## Key Claims

1. **Astringent compounds are nearly insoluble** at typical brew temperatures (≤90°C) — they exist as suspended undissolved particles in the slurry, not as dissolved compounds. They require very high temperatures to dissolve efficiently.
2. **Astringency = filtration problem**: a thick, flat, undisturbed coffee bed with slow, even water flow physically filters out suspended astringent particles. Filtration fails when flow is uneven, bypassed, or too fast (due to pressure).
3. **Slurry saturation doesn't help**: a 10-minute steep brings the slurry to saturation of dissolved compounds, but undissolved astringent particles are still present. Uneven drawdown flow still lets them through. (Trinity One observation — fine without sampling at top, astringent after drawdown)
4. **Grind size ceiling**: finer grind → more broken cells exposed → more astringent compounds liberated into slurry (proportional to total particle surface area). Beyond a certain grind size, even a thick, flat, perfectly filtered bed cannot remove all liberated particles. Fine-grind experiments on the SOL dripper with paper filter + thick bed + no bypass + apparently even flow still produced astringency at near-espresso grind sizes. (Gagné & Thibaut Paggen, Canadian Roasting Society experiment)
5. **Pressure → more astringency**: higher brew pressure → faster microscopic water flow between particles → more efficient dragging of undissolved particles through the bed. This explains why: espresso is inherently astringent (9 bar); hard Aeropress pressing produces more astringency and cloudiness than a gentle press (Hoffman and Gagné confirmed).
6. **Low-temperature strategy (tested at 70°C)**: lower slurry temperature → lower astringent compound solubility → smaller fraction dissolves and fewer undissolved particles liberated. Results: EY ~27% with no perceptible astringency, much finer grind possible. But cup profile was significantly less interesting — "earthy, chocolaty, nutty, vegetal," less vibrant acidity. Scott Rao: "Oh yes, I noticed this years ago but the cup profiles were always boring." Decent machine testers with drastically-falling temperature profiles confirmed: high EY, not vibrant.
7. **The specific chemical**: not named. As of August 2022, withheld pending publication by Smrke's colleagues. NOT wine tannins — those were searched for in coffee and not found in significant quantities (Barista Hustle reference). Smrke communicated the compound's identity to Gagné privately.
8. **Decaf observation**: decaffeinated beans appear nearly immune to astringency. The decaffeination process (water, CO₂, or solvent) may affect or destroy the specific astringent compound. Consistent with the filtration model: if decaf processing neutralizes the compound, decaf beans liberate fewer astringent particles regardless of grind size.
9. **~95°C upper threshold**: Gagné suspects that ~95°C is the point above which brews become unavoidably astringent regardless of technique, based on Hario siphon observations (maintaining slurry consistently above 95°C always produced poor cups).
10. **Long contact time + fine grind subtlety**: long contact times may draw out slightly more astringency via an inefficient dissolution-precipitation cycle — an astringent compound may dissolve, exit a coffee cell, then precipitate out, gradually transferring material from intact cells to the slurry. Relevant for long-steep filter methods with fine grinds. (Rao observation; Gagné's mechanism)
11. **Smrke's caveat (post-publication comment)**: Smrke noted he is not certain undissolved compounds IN THE CUP contribute to the sensation — they may need to dissolve in the cup. But filtration still controls total quantity of astringent compound in the cup, which controls what can dissolve — so the mechanism holds either way.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Tension with Rao's CGA model**: Rao attributes underdevelopment astringency to excess chlorogenic acid (CGA). CGA is water-soluble — it would dissolve and pass through the coffee bed like other solubles, and would NOT be filtered by Gagné's mechanism. If both models are correct, these represent two distinct astringency mechanisms: filtration-based (undissolved unknown compound) and dissolved-compound (CGA → salivary protein interaction). Open question.
- **The specific compound identity**: withheld in August 2022 pending peer review. Whether a paper has since been published identifying the compound is unresolved in this wiki.
- **Quaker mechanism ambiguity**: Rao describes quakers as contributing "suspended astringent particles" but the underlying chemistry of immature beans may differ from normal beans. Whether quaker-sourced astringency is the same undissolved-particle pathway or a different mechanism is unclear.
- The 70°C experiment was limited to Gagné and a small number of testers on a single coffee. Does the flavor degradation extend to all coffees/roasts or only some?
