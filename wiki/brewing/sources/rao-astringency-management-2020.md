# Managing Astringency in Coffee Brewing

## Summary

Scott Rao's 2020 practical guide to diagnosing and fixing astringency. He identifies polyphenols — primarily chlorogenic acids (CGAs) and tannins — as the likely primary astringent compounds in brewed coffee, noting that they are **large molecules that extract less readily** than most other coffee solubles. Three risk factors are identified: underripe cherry, underdevelopment in roasting, and channeling during percolation. The core practical contribution is a five-step diagnostic protocol to isolate which cause is responsible in a given cup. Written before Gagné's 2022 filtration model; by 2025, Rao's language has evolved to adopt Gagné's suspended-particle framing.

## Key Claims

1. **Primary astringent compounds**: polyphenols — in particular CGAs and tannins. "Large molecules" that "tend to extract less readily than most other coffee solubles."
2. **CGA vs. tannins**: CGAs seem to extract more readily than tannins. (Rao's qualification: "It has not been scientifically proven... but it seems probable.") Therefore CGA is likely the dominant astringent compound in most brewing situations.
3. **Majority via channeling**: "It's likely the majority of astringent polyphenols found in brewed coffee extract via channeling in percolation brewing."
4. **Immersion vs. percolation**: "Astringency is much less likely to occur in immersion brewing due to the lack of channeling." (Qualified by later evidence: quakers produce astringency even in cupping bowls — Rao 2025; and Gagné 2022 showed even saturated long-steep slurries produce astringency during uneven drawdown. The 2020 claim is accurate for channeling-sourced astringency specifically, not for all astringency sources.)
5. **Three risk factors**: underripe cherry; underdevelopment in roasting; channeling during percolation.
6. **CGA linkage for both underripe and underdeveloped**: "Both underripe cherry and underdeveloped roasts tend to yield higher levels of CGAs in the cup. CGAs are both astringent polyphenols and also the most prevalent acids in coffee." Having some CGA is important for a delicious cup — it's excess CGA that causes perceptible astringency.

## Diagnostic Protocol (Five-Step Decision Tree)

| Step | Observation | Conclusion | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Previous brew of same roast batch was NOT astringent | Channeling caused the current astringency | Fix technique or grind coarser |
| 2 | Cannot rule out channeling (untrusted method/skill) | Unknown | Cup the coffee (immersion); if cupping is non-astringent → channeling was the cause |
| 3 | Both percolation AND cupping (immersion) are astringent | Not (or not only) channeling | Cause is underdevelopment and/or underripe cherry |
| 4 | Some roast batches of the coffee are astringent, others aren't | Roast development is the variable | Fix roasting: more development |
| 5 | ALL roast batches astringent when cupped, and you are confident they are not underdeveloped | Green coffee quality issue | Likely underripe cherry; address at sourcing stage |

**Practical priority**: the protocol distinguishes between brewing technique (fixable immediately), roasting (fixable in the next batch), and green coffee quality (requires sourcing change — no brew or roast fix will help).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Tannin claim partially superseded**: Gagné 2022 reports that Smrke's colleagues have likely identified the specific astringent compound, and wine tannins were searched for in coffee brews and NOT found in significant quantities. The "tannins" part of Rao's 2020 claim is likely outdated. CGA as a candidate remains plausible.
- **CGA reconciliation with Gagné**: Rao's characterization of CGA as a "large molecule that extracts less readily" is actually consistent with Gagné's 2022 filtration model (nearly insoluble = partly undissolved = filterable). The apparent tension between "CGA is water-soluble" and "astringency is a filtration problem" is partially resolved: CGA's slow/incomplete dissolution at typical brew temperatures may leave enough undissolved fraction to be filterable. See [[shared/astringency]] for the full resolution.
- **Immersion claim partially qualified**: Rao's 2020 claim that immersion brewing is "much less likely" to cause astringency holds for channeling-sourced astringency. It does not hold for quaker-sourced astringency (quakers cause astringency even in cupping — Rao 2025) or for uneven drawdown from a saturated immersion steep (Gagné 2022, Trinity One observation).
- This source predates Gagné's 2022 filtration model by ~2 years. Rao's 2025 language ("suspended particles," "nylon filter evidence") reflects evolution beyond this 2020 framing.
