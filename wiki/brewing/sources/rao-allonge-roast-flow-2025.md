# Scott Rao Masterclass — Allongé vs. Standard Espresso: Roast Level, Flow Rate & Flavor

**Source**: Scott Rao (SR) and John Buckman (JB). Decent Espresso masterclass. YouTube transcript. 2025-08-24.
**Credibility**: **High-Medium (practitioner)** — same tier as [[brewing/sources/rao-blooming-filter3-quakers-2025]] (6 days later in same event series). Transport/diffusion framework attributed to Jonathan Gagné (author of "The Physics of Filter Coffee") — second-hand. Roast weight loss → brew ratio table is Rao's empirical synthesis, not peer-reviewed. No COI beyond Decent Espresso affiliation.

**Coffee served**: Tesoro Washed Typica; Rao's roast at 11% mass loss ("Nordic" level; at the edge of development — "I don't think we can go much lighter without having grassy, hay-like flavors due to underdevelopment").

## Summary

A mechanistic talk on how flow rate interacts with roast level to determine espresso flavor character. Primary deliverable: a roast weight-loss → optimal brew ratio decision table connecting roasting and brewing domains. Secondary contributions: the transport vs. diffusion extraction framework; extraction potential ceiling by roast level; Allongé and Turbo shot as high-flow strategies; deep bed and paper filter as channeling mitigations; Rao's single-variable dialing-in philosophy.

## Key Claims

### Roast Weight Loss → Optimal Brew Ratio (Cross-Domain Decision Table)

Rao presents a framework for selecting brew ratio based on roast degree as measured by mass loss:

| Roast category | Approx. mass loss | Optimal brew ratio (30s shot) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nordic (extreme light) | ~11% | 4:1 – 5:1 | At edge of development; Allongé or Filter3 needed |
| Light/Medium (typical Third Wave) | 12–13% | 3:1 | Standard specialty espresso approach |
| Medium | 14–15% | 2:1 | Standard espresso ratio; some roast character; milk blends |
| Dark | 16–18% | 1:1 (ristretto) | Heading into second crack territory |
| Very Dark (Starbucks) | ~20% | — ("just don't") | Beyond practical specialty range |

**Mechanism**: Higher mass loss = more bitter-sweet compounds in the bean. Higher flow rate/longer ratio → more bitters extracted. Solution: stop shots shorter (ristretto) for darker roasts. Conversely, lighter roasts have fewer bitters; can run more water through the puck before undesirable flavors emerge — and benefit from high flow to emphasize fruit acids.

**Connection to Lindsey 2024**: Mass loss labels align with and extend the sourced thresholds in [[roasting/concepts/roasting-parameters]] — Lindsey found EY peaks at 12–14% and caffeine peaks at 14–16%. Rao's brewing ratio recommendations are consistent with these chemical inflection points.

**Connection to underdevelopment threshold**: Rao's 11% ("barely not grassy") converges with Lindsey's <12% "underdeveloped" label from extraction chemistry. Two independent data points (sensory and chemical) confirming the same ~12% lower boundary.

### Flow Rate × Roast Level — The Core Mechanism

**Light roasts (high flow is better)**:
- High flow rate emphasizes fruit acids; "tones down caramels and bitters"
- Allongé at 4.5 ml/sec can feel "borderline sour, wine-y" — intentionally pushing fruit acids
- Lighter roasts: fruit acids are the main flavor story; high flow extracts them preferentially

**Dark roasts (high flow is worse)**:
- Bitter-sweet compounds in dark roasts are "a bit larger, tend to take longer to dissolve"
- High flow or long shots bring out "maybe a little bit too much of these bitter sweets"
- Shorter shots (ristretto) + lower flow + lower temperature = management strategy

**Regional Italian pattern (empirical validation)**:
- Northern Italy: lighter roasts, longer shots, higher flow rates (lighter → longer is correct)
- Southern Italy: darker roasts, shorter shots (ristretto), lower flow (darker → shorter is correct)
- "They've kind of worked this out instinctively" — Rao treats this as validation of the framework

**Mechanism**: Not fully understood chemically ("we're not necessarily sure why this is"). Likely: fruit acids are lighter molecules that come out faster at high flow; bitter-sweet compounds (larger molecules) are extracted at a slower rate and accumulate more in longer/higher-flow shots.

**Practical rule**: Match flow rate to roast degree. Light roast → higher flow. Dark roast → lower flow, shorter shot.

### Extraction Potential by Roast Level

Common belief: "Dark roasts are easier to extract." Rao's correction:

"A well-developed roast is easier to extract than an underdeveloped roast — true. But once you're in the range of what I would call 'developed', extraction potential tends to peak at a relatively light/medium roast."

Dark roasts do become more porous (easier to extract some compounds), but roasting has also burned off soluble material. Result: higher percentage of unextractable cellulose, lower proportion of soluble compounds by weight. A Starbucks-level roast would extract *lower* EY than a light roast from the same green coffee.

**Connection to Lindsey 2024**: This precisely matches the EY peak at 12-14% mass loss, declining EY above that — Lindsey showed this empirically; Rao provides the intuitive mechanism (burned-off solubles).

### Transport vs. Diffusion (Jonathan Gagné, cited second-hand)

Two mechanisms of extraction in coffee:

**Transport / Erosion**: Water physically strips surface solubles from ground particles as it flows past. Dominant mechanism in standard espresso. Fast flow → more transport.

**Diffusion**: Solubles from deeper within the cellular structure of the grounds dissolve into the surrounding liquid and migrate outward. Dominant in immersion brewing (French Press, cupping bowl, Blooming during the pause). Slow, requires contact time.

**Allongé and diffusion**: Because so much solvent runs through the puck (4:1–5:1 ratio), Allongé may include more diffusion than standard espresso. Rao: "we're getting an exaggeration of the fruit acids at the expense of the bitter sweets" — possibly because diffusion-extracted compounds have different flavor profiles from transport-extracted ones. Not yet confirmed by GC/MS analysis.

**Blooming**: The 30-second pause is specifically a diffusion phase. Rao: "you get a lot of time to dissolve stuff [during the pause], and then...you have a pretty high flow rate" in percolation. Blooming explicitly combines diffusion (pause) and transport (high-flow percolation).

### Specific Allongé Parameters

- **Flow rate**: 4.5 ml/sec (constant flow) — "that's pretty fast for espresso"
- **EY**: 26–27% typical
- **Ratio**: 4:1–5:1
- "Really highlights acidity and fruity flavors in a way that even filter coffee can't do"
- Higher astringency risk: running 100g through 20g puck; channeling risk; deep bed helps
- Rao's Montreal café experience: updosed to create a deep bed; separate grinder with separate setting for Allongé; careful puck prep routine; found they "really liked the Allongé" — validates the technique at commercial scale

### Blooming — Additional Parameters

- Percolation phase flow rate: ~4 ml/sec
- Pressure peaks: ~9 bar
- Viscosity/density: more like a 2:1 shot despite high EY — "comes out rather viscous"
- EY: up to 29–30% (confirmed in previous source)

### Turbo Shot

A shorter version of the Allongé: high flow rate, but ratio stopped earlier than Allongé.

**Use case**: Machines/setups where puck prep is not perfect and Allongé's long run would amplify channeling → astringency. Turbo shot gets the high-flow flavor benefit without the channeling risk of long water volume. Mentioned Turbo Turbo variant at ~3.5 ml/sec.

**Pattern**: "A lot of people have gone from Allongé to using Turbo shots" — acknowledges that Allongé is ideal but demanding.

### Channeling Prevention Techniques

**Deep bed / updosing**: Deeper puck = harder for channels to reach the bottom of the bed and extract astringent particles. Rao used this at his Montreal café for Allongé production.

**Paper filter tips** (top or bottom of puck):
- **Bottom**: Allows slightly finer grind; modest channeling benefit
- **Top** (more effective): Water pools above the top filter before passing through → more uniform initial wetting vs. jet-based spot wetting from shower screen. Can help significantly on machines with poor shower screen distribution.
- New Freckles/Dimples shower screens (Decent) serve the same purpose as a top paper filter — even distribution → uniform wetting

### Dialing-In Philosophy (Rao)

Keep the recipe constant across different coffees. Only adjust:
1. **Grind setting** — to manage flow rate and extraction dynamics
2. **Dilution at end** — if TDS is too high for preference (especially for high-extracting Kenya/Ethiopia)

"I wouldn't change my flow rate or something like that because of it. I just accept that some coffees are going to extract higher than others."

Benefit: avoids getting "lost" from changing multiple variables simultaneously. Simpler to return to standard and troubleshoot.

**TDS target (filter coffee)**: Rao's personal preference: 1.35–1.4 TDS for filter coffee — "strong enough to be pleasing but weak enough to differentiate lots of flavors."

### Ethiopia/Kenya vs. Colombia/Guatemala Extraction

"Your typical Kenya or Ethiopia will extract quite a bit higher than your typical Colombian or Guatemalan." No mechanism given; accepted as empirical observation. Implication: same recipe will produce higher TDS from East African coffees; adjust via dilution rather than recipe change.

Possible connections: Ethiopian/Kenyan landrace varieties typically grown at higher altitude (denser beans → potentially different cell structure); washed process may influence; not yet sourced in this wiki.

### 25-30 Second Shot Timing Zone

Empirical pattern: "whether you pull a ristretto, whether you pull an Allongé or anything in-between, you end up roughly near 30 seconds, and every time you stray too far from that, it's not too great."

JB: timing-based flow profiling (vary flow to achieve target time) is being used by multiple companies with good results. May be equivalent to Decent's Adaptive profile approach with a different metric.

Not yet sourced in peer-reviewed literature; treat as practitioner observation.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Transport vs. diffusion in Allongé**: Rao acknowledges "no one's bothered doing GC of different flow rates" — the flavor difference (more fruit acids at high flow) is confirmed sensorially but the chemical mechanism (transport vs. diffusion differences) is unconfirmed.
- **Kenya/Ethiopia extracting higher**: stated as an empirical universal but no mechanism is given. Could relate to altitude/density, varietal, or processing method patterns. Not yet sourced in this wiki.
- **"25-30 second zone"**: interesting pattern with no mechanistic explanation. Filed as practitioner observation.
