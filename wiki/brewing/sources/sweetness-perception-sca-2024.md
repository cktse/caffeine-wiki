# How Sweet Coffee Tastes! Towards an Understanding of Coffee Sweetness

**Authors**: Dr. Nancy Cordoba, Dr. Devin Peterson, Peter Giuliano
**Published**: SCA "25" Magazine, Issue 22, 2024-10-07
**Institution**: Flavor Research and Education Center, Ohio State University (research); Coffee Science Foundation (funder)
**Credibility**: High — SCA authoritative publication; credible academic institution; 125 professional coffee tasters; LC-MS chemical analysis; proper statistical methods. **Caveat**: this is a magazine article reporting *intermediate* results of an ongoing 2-year project (launched 2023). The final peer-reviewed paper identifying specific sweet-tasting compounds (Hypothesis 5, "flavoromics") is not yet published.

## Summary

Reports early results of a Coffee Science Foundation project at Ohio State University investigating what causes the perception of sweetness in black coffee. Five hypotheses tested. Core finding: coffee sweetness is NOT caused by sugars (all sugars are below human sensory threshold in brewed coffee). Sweetness is instead a multifactorial phenomenon driven by (1) volatile aromatic compounds perceived via retronasal/orthonasal pathways, and (2) unknown nonvolatile non-sugar compounds tasted on the tongue. Sweetness perception is also modulated by other flavor signals: fruity/fermented aromas enhance it; bitter/roasted flavors suppress it. The specific non-sugar sweet-tasting compounds are under investigation via flavoromics.

## Five Hypotheses and Results

| Hypothesis | Result |
|---|---|
| 1. Coffee sweetness is real and perceptible | ✓ Confirmed — 6-point range across samples on a 15-point scale; 125 professional tasters agree on ranking |
| 2. Sweetness is an aromatic phenomenon | ✓ Partially — nose clips reduced range from 6 to 3.6 points; rankings preserved → aromas matter but nonvolatile compounds also play a role |
| 3. Could it be sugars after all? | ✗ Confirmed not sugars — all sugars below sensory threshold; coffees with highest sugars were least sweet |
| 4. Sweetness modulated by other flavors | ✓ Confirmed — fruity/fermented/floral → enhanced sweet; bitter/roasted/papery → suppressed sweet |
| 5. Non-sugar sweet-tasting compounds exist | Pending — flavoromics work ongoing as of Oct 2024 |

## Key Claims

- Sucrose sensory threshold: ~2,000 mg/L; tested coffees maxed at ~100 mg/L — far below perception
- Medium roast retains only 0.9% of green coffee sucrose; light roast retains 2.9% — but even light roast is below the threshold. Sweetness is NOT explained by sugar retention at any roast level.
- The coffees with highest measured sugars were the LEAST sweet — paradox that points to another variable (likely roast degree correlating with both sugar retention AND bitterness/roasted notes that suppress sweetness)
- Sweetest coffees: associated with fruity, fermented aromas and floral aromatics (vanilla)
- Least sweet coffees: associated with bitter taste, roasted/papery flavors, and the highest sugar levels
- Removing aroma (nose clips) cut the sweetness differentiation range in half but did not change which coffees ranked sweetest vs. least sweet — both mechanisms (aromatic and nonvolatile) operate simultaneously
- Sweetness has been valued in coffee for centuries; only ~40 years the industry incorrectly attributed it to sugar; 95% of surveyed experts (2021 SCA study) agreed the 2004 Cupping Protocol underweights sweetness

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- The specific nonvolatile non-sugar compounds responsible for sweet taste on the tongue are NOT yet identified — this is a fundamental open question in coffee science
- The study examined sweetness in roasted/brewed coffee without controlling for processing method or roast level; the mechanism connecting processing choices (e.g., CO₂ fermentation → fruity/fermented aromatics) to sweetness perception is implied but not directly tested here
- Clarke & Macrae (1985) sugar degradation data is well-established, but the study doesn't explain the causal pathway from roast bitterness to sweetness suppression
- 125 professional tasters may not represent consumer perception; trained tasters may perceive coffee differently
