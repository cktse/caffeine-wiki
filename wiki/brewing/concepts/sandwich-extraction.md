# Sandwich Extraction (Particle-Size Stratification)

## Definition

A pour-over structuring technique (Bavis Kwong, 2026 WBrC 3rd) that **stratifies the coffee bed by particle size** so each fraction does a different job, instead of chasing one uniform grind. Coarse grounds sit on **top** (percolation, higher agitation/temp → bright acidity + florals); a thin layer of **fines** sits on the **bottom**, isolated between two fast filter papers (delayed start, low agitation → near-immersion → sweetness, body, roundness). The insight: **fines are reframed from a defect into a controllable structural tool.** It's a *competition* technique — the value for daily brewing is the reasoning, not the exact numbers.

## The structure — 3 functions, not a shape

```
1st paper → 2g FINES → 2nd paper → 14g COARSE   (water enters at the top)
```

| Layer | Material | Extraction environment | Sensory job | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Top** | 14g coarse | percolation, higher agitation/temp | jasmine, white grape, peach, bright acidity | too coarse / too-gentle pour → thin, sharp, short |
| **Middle** | 2nd fast paper (Sibarist FAST) | isolates the two size fractions; maintains flow path | stops fines remixing into the top bed | slow paper here → double layer stalls |
| **Bottom** | 2g fines (~12.5%) | delayed, low-agitation, near-immersion | honey sweetness, medium body, roundness | too much / too long / too hot → bitter, dry, choked |

The bottom is **not** a sealed immersion chamber — water still passes through; it's simply lower-agitation, paper-isolated, and later-starting than the top.

## Why coarse on top, fines on the bottom

In normal brewing, fines are trouble: high surface area, fast dissolution, migration to the bottom, rising resistance, and a slowing tail — so fines often turn dry/bitter *before* the coarse is fully extracted. Sandwich extraction doesn't discard them; it **sieves them out (900μm) and pins a fixed 2g to the bottom** — changing fines from a variable scattered through the bed into a layer with controlled **weight, position, and start time**.

## The dry bloom is a timing device (its real purpose)

The **15g** bloom onto **14g** top coarse (~1:1) is sized to **stay in the top and not reach the fines**. Fines extract the instant they wet, so if the bloom penetrates, the bottom steeps ~40s longer — ~20% of a 3:30 brew — enough to push sweetness into bitterness. So the "dry bloom" here is **to delay the bottom's extraction start**, not to degas. (Cozy House reports this as the highest-failure step for students.)

## The dripper as a thermal-gradient device

The Contour Drip ([[brewing/entities/contour-drip]]) pairs a **ceramic top** (heat-retentive → drive coarse acidity/vitality) with a **silicone base** (less retentive → spare the bottom fines from prolonged high heat). This extends "water temperature" from a single kettle number into a **position-dependent** thermal environment. Caveat: at home, don't attribute the whole effect to material — preheat, room temp, pour speed, bed height, and wait times all move heat around. A [[brewing/entities/hario-switch|Hario Switch]] is a close accessible substitute (its valve directly controls the bottom layer's contact time).

## Roast ↔ brew are co-designed (the cross-domain lesson)

The comp bean was roasted to **extreme short development** (first crack ~8:30, **DTR ~10% → ~57s** development — vs a typical specialty 15–22% DTR). That maximises acid complexity and volatile florals but leaves **Maillard sweetness (caramel/nut/cocoa) undeveloped** — a deliberate "bet on acidity," with underdevelopment green-ness as the risk. The **bottom-fines immersion is the extraction structure that adds the sweetness back.** Roast curve and brew structure are one system, not two — see [[roasting/concepts/roasting-parameters]] (DTR, DT-sweetness mechanism).

## Water

Low-mineral **~35ppm, pH 6** water keeps high-Geisha acidity direct and clear (lower buffering → less acid-flattening). This is **deliberately below** the SCA brewing range (50–175ppm, target ~68ppm) — a targeted sensory choice, not a universal one. And ppm/pH alone don't determine extraction: **alkalinity, Ca/Mg hardness, and ion composition** matter too. Practical home test: hold bean + recipe fixed, compare two waters of different mineral content. *(No standalone water concept in the wiki yet — open item.)*

## The recipe (illustration, as-claimed)

16g (2g fines / 14g coarse, 900μm) · two Sibarist FAST papers ([[brewing/concepts/fast-filters]]) · 94–95 °C · ~35ppm/pH6 · dry bloom 15g ~40s (top only) · **4 × 50g** every 40s (0:40 / 1:20 / 2:00 / 2:40) · finish ~3:25–3:30 → **1:13.4**.

> **215g vs 250g:** the 215g version (15 + 4×50) is internally consistent and matches the finalist's "16g : 215ml"; the 250g record implies a different pour and reads thin (1:15.6). Start from **215g and adjust with [[brewing/concepts/bypass|bypass]]** rather than changing the layer structure.

## What to actually take home (Cozy House)

1. **Grind distribution isn't just "even vs uneven."** Coarse, body, and fines have different extraction speeds; understanding their roles beats chasing one pretty average particle size.
2. **Write the sensory task first, then the operation.** Bavis's short-development roast (acidity), 35ppm water (clarity), and bottom immersion (sweetness) all point the same way — chain consistency, not single-parameter optimisation.
3. **A complex step must justify itself.** If removing a step doesn't change something identifiable in the cup, it's just imitation. Also: this is a **competition** technique (90s–2min prep; a 0.3g miss reshapes the bottom), **not** an operational one.

## Common failure modes
Too much bottom fines (>~2g → stall/bitter) · mid paper floats up and remixes (press it down / wet the edge) · papers too slow (double layer stalls) · bloom penetrates to the fines (over-steeps bottom) · top too coarse (florals don't open, front/back like two cups) · watching total time instead of flow rate · water too hard / too alkaline (blurs white-grape/citrus acidity).
