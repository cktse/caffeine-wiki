# The Art and Science of Coffee Roasting: Fundamentals, Control, and Technological Innovations

## Summary

Comprehensive book chapter providing scientific and practical foundations of coffee roasting: six-phase process framework with temperature markers, physicochemical reactions (Maillard, caramelization, CGA degradation, lipid interactions), equipment types with sourced sensory implications, process control variables, and emerging trends. Published in "Coffee Post-Harvest: History, Science, Processing, Roasting and Applications" (Paula Editorações, ISBN 978-65-5262-284-6). Primarily a synthesis chapter with 40+ peer-reviewed references (journals: Food Chemistry, LWT, J. Food Science, Beverages; most recent: 2025). Used in this wiki as the **structural anchor for the roasting domain** — provides the temperature framework and chemical mechanism overview assumed as background by the narrower ingests (Rao 2020, Alstrup 2020, Lindsey 2024).

## Key Claims

### Green Coffee Composition

Green coffee baseline: **10–12% moisture**; complex matrix of sugars, lipids, proteins, chlorogenic acids (CGAs), and minerals (Gonzalez & Ramirez-Mares 2014; Chu et al. 2009). Physical: dense, hard, low porosity, compact cellular structure — makes uniform heat penetration difficult. Dense beans require longer heat exposure to achieve the same roast degree as less dense beans.

### Six-Phase Temperature Framework

| Phase | Temp range | Key physical events | Key chemistry |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Drying** | ~25°C → 120°C | Moisture evaporates; color turns yellowish; bread/cereal aroma | Endothermic; water removal only; no significant flavor reactions |
| **2. Initial Browning** | ~120°C → 150°C | Brown color develops; roasted aroma emerges | Maillard begins (reducing sugars + amino acids); caramelization starts; hundreds of aroma intermediates form |
| **3. First Crack** | ~150°C → 185°C; 1C at **175–185°C BT** | Audible popping; bean expands volumetrically; volatile release accelerates | Endothermic → exothermic transition; CO₂/water vapor pressure exceeds bean structural strength; onset of full flavor and aroma development |
| **4. Development** | After 1C → drop | Color deepens to target; full roast aroma develops | Maillard + caramelization continue intensely; DT and DTR are measured here; most critical stage |
| **5. Second Crack** | ~**225–235°C** | Second audible pop; cell walls rupture again; surface oils released; stronger toasted aroma | Partial carbonization begins; key volatiles degraded; "not recommended in roasting processes where quality preservation is the objective" |
| **6. Cooling** | Immediate post-drop | Rapid temperature drop stops chemical reactions | CO₂ degassing begins; aromatic compounds stabilize; 24h+ needed before grinding/brewing |

The critical quality window: between Phase 3 (first crack) and Phase 5 (second crack). All specialty roasting exists in this range; discharge timing within Phase 4 determines roast level.

### Chemical Reactions

- **Maillard reaction**: begins ~120°C, extends to ~180°C; reducing sugars + amino acids → melanoidins (brown color) + volatile compounds including **aldehydes, ketones, pyrazines, furans** (sweet, floral, nutty, roasted notes). Excessive heating degrades aromatic compounds → burnt/bitter.
- **Medium roast specifics**: intensified Maillard produces **pyrazines and furans** specifically → **chocolate, caramel, nutty, and hazelnut notes** (Debona et al. 2020, cited in chapter).
- **Caramelization**: above 160°C; sucrose → furans + dihydrofuranones; reduces acidity, enhances sweetness; excessive → bitterness.
- **CGA decomposition**: CGA (up to 10% of green coffee mass) → **caffeic acid and lactones** (phenolic derivatives); influences body, astringency, and aftertaste.
- **Lipid × nitrogen compound interactions**: vast array of volatiles — aldehydes, phenols, pyrazines, sulfur compounds; **>800 identified volatile substances** in roasted coffee total (Barrios-Rodríguez et al. 2021).
- **Physical changes**: bean expansion, density reduction, increased porosity; cellular structure becomes fragile and porous (favors aroma retention/diffusion); color evolves from green to dark brown.

### Process Variables

| Variable | Function | Extreme effects |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Governs Maillard + caramelization rate | Too fast → surface scorching, underdeveloped interior; too slow → volatile degradation, flat-tasting beverage |
| Airflow | Convective heat transfer + removal of gases and particulates | Controls heat uniformity; flavor clarity |
| Time (combined with temperature curve) | Defines acidity/body/bitterness balance | Within the thermal trajectory shaped by all other variables |

### Roast Level Sensory Mapping (sourced)

| Roast level | Key sensory characteristics |
|---|---|
| **Light** | Pronounced acidity; fruity/floral aromas preserved; high phenolic content + antioxidant potential; market: North America, Nordic |
| **Medium** | Balance point (acidity/sweetness/bitterness); pyrazines + furans dominant → **chocolate, caramel, nutty, hazelnut**; most preferred in specialty consumer research |
| **Dark** | Reduced acidity; intensified bitterness; smoky/burnt caramel/toasted notes; oily surface; heavy body; may mask defects in lower-quality beans |

### Equipment Type and Sensory Impact (sourced)

| Roaster type | Heat transfer | Cup character tendency |
|---|---|---|
| **Drum** | Conduction + convection | "Slower, more controlled roast; **greater body and depth**" |
| **Fluidized bed** | Convection only | "Faster, more uniform roast; **accentuates fruit and acidity notes**" |
| Infrared/microwave | Radiation-dominant | More sustainable; energy-efficient; emerging; limited specialty data |

### Bean Density and Roasting

Denser beans (higher altitude, harder varietals) require longer heat exposure to achieve the same roast degree as less dense beans. Density varies by species, variety, and growing conditions. Practical implication: high-altitude specialty coffees (Chiroso at 1,950–2,100m; Geisha at 1,400–2,000m Panama) need different roast profiles vs. lower-altitude varieties.

### Emerging Trends

AI/precision roasting; green roasting (heat recovery, emissions reduction); roast customization by bean origin and variety; by-product repurposing (chaff, particulates → bioenergy); Artisan/Cropster-type software as standard.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Temperature markers are indicative: density, size, hardness, and growing conditions all affect thermal behavior. The 175–185°C first crack range is typical but not universal.
- **"Excessively slow → volatile degradation → flat"** (Lee et al. 2024 via this chapter) and **"hard RoR crash → baked defect"** (Rao 2020) are related but distinct claims. Slow roasting degrades volatiles; RoR crashes cause baking defects. These may produce different sensory profiles that happen to overlap in "flat/dull" character.
- Water quench cooling effect: Baggenstoss 2007 (on degassing and aroma stability post-water-quench) cited but not detailed. Preferred cooling method not established in this wiki.
- The synthesis character means all numerical claims reference other sources. No original measurements from this chapter.
