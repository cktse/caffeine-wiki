# Coffee Sweetness — Sensory Mechanism

## The Core Mystery

Black coffee is widely perceived as sweet — yet scientific analysis shows roasted coffee contains almost no sugar. Medium-roast coffee retains only 0.9% of the sucrose present in green beans; light roast retains 2.9%. Even at light roast, this produces concentrations far below the human sensory threshold for sweetness (sucrose threshold ~2,000 mg/L; brewed coffee maxes out at ~100 mg/L).

**Coffee's sweetness is not caused by sugar.** It has been known since at least 1985 (Clarke & Macrae) that sugars are below threshold. The specialty coffee industry spent 40 years building cupping systems (e.g., the 2004 SCA Cupping Protocol) on an incorrect assumption.

## What Actually Causes Sweetness

Coffee sweetness is a multifactorial phenomenon with two simultaneous mechanisms (Cordoba, Peterson & Giuliano, SCA/OSU, 2024):

**1. Aromatic compounds (volatile)**
- Perceived via both orthonasal (through the nose) and retronasal (rising from the throat) pathways
- When nose clips were applied to trained tasters, sweetness perception dropped — the range of perceived sweetness fell from 6 points to 3.6 points on a 15-point scale
- Rankings of coffees by sweetness remained similar with and without nose clips → aromas contribute significantly but do not explain sweetness alone

**2. Nonvolatile non-sugar compounds (taste on tongue)**
- A second mechanism operates in the mouth independently of aroma
- The specific compounds are not yet identified — ongoing flavoromics research at Ohio State University
- These may function similarly to non-sugar sweeteners (stevia, aspartame): compounds that stimulate sweet taste receptors without being sugar

## Sweetness Modulation

Sweetness perception is actively shaped by other flavors present in the cup. This is flavor modulation: the brain integrates all taste and aroma signals and constructs a "flavor image" of sweetness (or anti-sweetness) — not just a direct response to sweet-tasting compounds.

| What is in the cup | Effect on perceived sweetness |
|---|---|
| Fruity, fermented aromas | **Enhances** sweetness perception |
| Floral aromatics (vanilla, jasmine) | **Enhances** sweetness perception |
| Bitter taste | **Suppresses** sweetness perception |
| Roasted, papery flavors | **Suppresses** sweetness perception |
| High sugar content (measured) | Paradoxically associated with **lower** sweetness perception — likely because high sugar retention correlates with under-developed roast profiles that also produce bitterness |

**The paradox**: coffees with the highest measured sugar concentrations in the OSU study were the LEAST sweet. This is the clearest evidence that sugar ≠ sweetness in coffee.

## Connection to Processing

This finding directly validates why CO₂-fermented natural coffees score higher on overall quality:

- CO₂ modified atmosphere fermentation (Moncayo et al. 2025) produced "aromatic, liquor-like, silky body" descriptors in natural coffee
- The OSU sweetness research identifies fruity/fermented aromatics as the primary aroma-category that enhances perceived sweetness
- **Together**: CO₂ fermentation → fruity/fermented aromatic profile → enhanced perceived sweetness → higher overall cup scores

This is not a coincidence — the two papers, from entirely different research teams and methodologies, converge on the same relationship between fermented aromatics and cup quality.

See [[processing/concepts/fermentation]] for the CO₂ fermentation evidence and [[processing/concepts/anaerobic-fermentation]] for the mechanism.

## Connection to Roasting

- Light roast retains more sugar (2.9% of green) and more volatile aromatic compounds — both mechanisms favor sweetness
- Dark roast produces more bitter compounds (caffeine liberated from protein complexes, Maillard degradation products) and more roasted/papery notes → suppresses sweetness via modulation
- **The implication**: sweetness in a dark roast requires more work from the cup's aromatics to overcome the anti-sweet effect of bitterness; the baseline is lower
- The intuition that light/medium roasts taste "sweeter" than dark is real and well-founded — but the mechanism is aromatics and bitterness suppression, not sugar content. Even light roast retains only 2.9% of green coffee sucrose, which remains below the sensory detection threshold. Dark roast has even less residual sugar, and compounds that more actively suppress sweetness.

### Specific sweetness compounds identified in roasted coffee (sourced: Alstrup et al. 2020)

A controlled DT study (Colombian washed, Agtron 76, French Press, 46 tasters, NMR + GC-MS) identified the specific compounds most strongly correlated with sweetness perception — and confirmed NO sugars were detectable above taste threshold in any sample:

| Compound | Sensory character | DT relationship | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2,3-Butanedione (diacetyl)** | Butter, caramel, butterscotch | **Peaks in fast DT (90s); degrades with extended DT** | Maillard/Strecker from sugar fragments |
| **2,3-Pentanedione** | Butter, caramel | Peaks in fast DT; degrades with extended DT | Maillard/Strecker from sugar fragments |
| Furfural | Sweet, woody | Highest in fast DT; declines with longer DT | Maillard from reducing sugars |
| Benzeneacetaldehyde | Honey, rose, floral | Highest in fast DT | Strecker degradation of phenylalanine |

**Actionable roasting implication**: within a given DTR window, a shorter total roast time — arriving at first crack faster, then a proportionally shorter absolute DT — will produce more diacetyl and 2,3-pentanedione, yielding a cup with higher sweetness perception. Extended development time destroys these compounds even if the DTR % remains constant.

**The specific anti-sweet roasting compounds**:
- **Pyrazines** (methylpyrazine, dimethylpyrazine, ethylmethylpyrazines): peak in slow/baked DT; roasty, nutty character; likely contribute to reduced sweetness by overwhelming the caramel/butter aromatics
- **Pyridine**: fishy/coffee aroma; monotonically increases with DT; proposed chemical marker for baked roast defect

**Cross-domain note — diacetyl's two pathways to the cup**:
Diacetyl arrives in the finished cup via two different upstream processes:
1. **Fermentation**: produced by lactic acid bacteria during extended post-harvest fermentation (documented in Chiroso extended fermentation — [[varietals/sources/chiroso-sensory-pazmino-2022]])
2. **Roasting**: produced via Maillard/Strecker degradation from sugar fragments during the development phase — highest at short DT

Both pathways contribute to the same buttery/caramel sweetness-enhancing aroma. A coffee with extended fermentation AND a fast DT roast may be maximally additive in diacetyl character.

## Connection to Brewing

- Extraction that preserves volatile aromatic compounds (lower temperature for light roasts, avoiding over-extraction) supports aromatic sweetness
- Over-extraction typically produces bitterness — which directly suppresses sweetness perception
- The 4:6 method's first-pour ratio control targets sweetness vs. brightness; this research suggests the mechanism is not via sugar extraction but via which aromatic/taste compounds are preferentially extracted
- Shorter extraction time may preserve fruity/floral aromatics that enhance sweetness; the tradeoff is soluble solids (body and strength)

**Cold brew and perceived sweetness**: a controlled study (Batali et al. 2022, UC Davis; see [[brewing/sources/cold-brew-vs-iced-batali-2022]]) identified four temperature-driven sensory differences — cold brew is more floral and less bitter/sour/rubbery. Sweetness is absent from this reported list, but the full 27-attribute sensory ballot is undisclosed and the SCA sweetness intensity scale did not exist until September 2023 (the study ran January–December 2021), so it is unclear whether sweetness was scored or how. **The study does not establish "cold brew is not sweeter."** What it does confirm — reduced bitterness and acidity — directly feeds into sweetness perception via the modulation mechanism established by this research: less bitterness = less sweetness suppression = perceived sweeter cup, even if sweet-tasting compounds are unchanged. Additionally, cold brewing Ethiopian washed coffee emphasizes fruity aromatics, which are sweetness-enhancing under this model. Cold brew may well feel sweeter to drinkers; the question is why.

**Independent corroboration (Guinard et al. 2023, UC Davis Brewing Control Chart)**: the new Sensory BCC ([[brewing/concepts/brewing-control-chart]]) places **'sweet'** — deliberately in quotes — in the low-TDS, low-extraction corner, explicitly because it is an *aromatic* flavor impression perceived in the **absence of bitterness and acidity**, not a sugar taste (Batali et al. 2020 monosaccharide analysis confirms sugars below threshold). This is a second research team, using descriptive + consumer sensory mapping rather than flavoromics, arriving at the same conclusion: coffee "sweetness" is aroma + the absence of suppressors, not sugar. It also gives a brewing-coordinate for sweetness — extract toward ~18% and keep strength modest.

See [[brewing/concepts/46-method]] for recipe parameters, [[brewing/concepts/v60]] for temperature guidance, [[brewing/concepts/cold-brew]] for cold brew specifics, [[brewing/concepts/brewing-control-chart]] for the TDS×PE sweetness coordinate.

## Connection to Varietals

- Ethiopian landraces (Yirgacheffe, Sidama) produce intense floral/citrus aromatics — these fall in the sweetness-enhancing category
- Kenyan SL28/SL34 produces blackcurrant/fruit aromatics — sweetness-enhancing
- Varietals with flat aromatic profiles, or profiles dominated by earthy/woody notes, will have less aromatic sweetness support
- This suggests varietal selection for sweetness is actually selection for aromatic complexity in the fruity/floral range, not for sugar content

## Scope and Caveats

- This is based on **intermediate results** of an ongoing 2-year project (published Oct 2024 = ~year 1). The final paper identifying specific non-sugar sweet-tasting compounds (Hypothesis 5, "flavoromics") is not yet available.
- The study used professional coffee tasters — consumer perception may differ
- Processing method, roast level, and varietal were **not controlled** in this study; the research isolated the sensory phenomenon itself rather than identifying the upstream variables
- The finding that "high-sugar coffees taste less sweet" is correlational — the causal mechanism (likely: roast profile variation) is not directly established

## Open Questions

- What specific nonvolatile non-sugar compounds create sweet taste on the tongue? (Flavoromics research ongoing — the key outstanding question in coffee sweetness science)
- How does cherry ripeness affect the aromatic precursors that create sweetness-enhancing flavors? (Explicitly out of scope for this study)
- Is the "high sugar = less sweet" correlation explained entirely by roast profile variation, or is there a direct inhibitory relationship?
- How do the sweetness-enhancing aromatics survive from green bean through roasting into the cup? Which roast parameters best preserve them? **Partially answered**: Alstrup 2020 shows diacetyl (2,3-butanedione) and 2,3-pentanedione are Maillard-derived (not green-bean origin) and peak at short DT within a given Agtron level. Fast DT within the DTR window = more sweetness compounds. Extended DT degrades them. The fermentation-derived diacetyl from processing (Chiroso study) adds to these roasting-derived compounds.
- Does the nose-clip residual sweetness (nonvolatile mechanism) vary by variety or processing more than by brewing parameters?
