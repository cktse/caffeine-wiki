# Quakers

## Definition

Quakers are coffee beans that developed from underripe cherries. Because the cherry did not fully mature, the seed received insufficient nutrition from the fruit — leaving it with lower sugar and flavor precursor content than fully mature beans.

**Spectrum**: from borderline-ripe (slightly early-picked) to severely immature (serious nutrition deficit). Severely immature quakers produce very bitter, harsh, vegetal, astringent flavor — a single quaker can destroy an otherwise excellent cup.

## Why They Matter

Quakers are one of three identified sources of astringency in brewed coffee (alongside channeling and severe underdevelopment), and the only one that originates entirely in the green coffee stage. Unlike channeling (a brewing technique problem) or underdevelopment (a roasting problem), quakers are a green bean sourcing quality issue that no amount of skilled roasting or brewing can fix.

The cup impact operates even in a no-channeling environment (cupping bowl, French Press): a single quaker gives a "dry sensation" that stops the aftertaste short — the oily, smooth finish characteristic of excellent coffee is interrupted. (Source: Rao, [[brewing/sources/rao-blooming-filter3-quakers-2025]])

## Identification (Post-Roast)

**Visual**: Lighter in color than surrounding beans; smoother in texture (mature beans have more surface texture/bumps).

**Smell test**: Smash a suspect bean on a hard surface and smell.
- Fruity aroma → NOT a quaker
- Nutty aroma → quaker

For each new coffee, Rao's protocol: sample roast → lay out on table → sort visuals → smell-test borderline beans → calibrate identifying characteristics for that specific lot (ripe color varies by tree variety and growing region).

## Processing Method and Quaker Prevalence

| Processing | Quaker removal | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Washed** | Natural float-sink step removes most quakers | Immature cherries are less dense → float in the washing channel; sunk = ripe = kept |
| **Natural (dried in cherry)** | No removal step | No water immersion → all cherries (ripe and unripe) proceed through drying together |
| **Honey** | Partial float removal (mucilage retained) | Some float-test occurs in some honey protocols; less systematic than washed |

**Implication for sourcing**: Natural coffees will typically contain more quakers than washed coffees from the same farm. If quaker prevalence is a concern for a lot under consideration, the processing method is a relevant variable.

## Root Cause

**Primary cause**: Inadequate soil nutrition, most commonly resulting from insufficient rainfall during tree development. Trees need rain to absorb fertilizer; drought → poor nutrient uptake → some cherries don't receive enough nutrition → quakers.

Quaker prevalence therefore correlates with growing conditions (dry years → more quakers on a given farm) and farm management practices (fertilization schedule, soil health). Cherry pickers cannot fully screen for quakers — they pick very fast, and the "ripe color" varies by tree variety, requiring manual calibration (hence pickers wearing painted wristbands matching the target shade for each tree type). Machine cherry sorting exists at some high-end farms.

## Sorting (Post-Roast)

### Manual / Experience-Based

Visual + smell identification during roast QC. Labor-intensive.

### Optical Sorting (Machine)

Beans fall in a curtain past front and back cameras. The machine:
1. Evaluates color of each individual bean
2. Assesses what percentage of the bean's surface fails the color threshold
3. Fires a compressed air jet to knock rejects out of the stream

Same technology used in rice and grain industries.

**Rao's approach (Decent Espresso)**: Removes ~20% of coffee by weight — far more than industry standard (<3% at most producers). Treated as equivalent to paying more for green coffee.

### Cost-Benefit Calibration (Rao Protocol)

5 cupping bowls: (1) unsorted, (2) fully sorted, (3–5) three intermediate levels. Presented blind with other coffees. Results establish the optimal sorting point — beyond which marginal improvement in cup does not justify the cost and product loss.

**Risk of over-sorting**: Beans just barely not-quakers can be the most intensely fruity beans in the lot (because they were nearly ripe; maximum sugar and aromatic precursor at the edge of ripeness). Sorting too aggressively risks removing these peak-quality beans along with genuine quakers.

## Cup Quality Impact

| Quaker severity | Cup impact |
|---|---|
| Borderline (barely underripe) | Slightly nutty off-note; may be masked in a complex cup |
| Moderate | Noticeable truncated aftertaste; dry sensation interrupts the finish |
| Severe (immature) | Bitter, harsh, vegetal, highly astringent; destroys the cup even at very small quantities |

**Astringency mechanism**: Quakers contribute astringent material independent of channeling. Whether quaker-sourced astringency operates via the same undissolved-particle filtration mechanism as channeling (Gagné 2022; see [[shared/astringency]]) or via a distinct dissolved-compound pathway is unclear — immature bean chemistry may differ meaningfully from normal beans. What is certain: quaker astringency cannot be fixed through brewing technique alone; it requires sorting.

**Decaffeination note**: The decaffeination process removes 90%+ of astringent compounds, which explains why decaff brews with high-percolation methods (Decent Filter2) are not astringent — the quaker-sourced astringent material is largely neutralized by decaffeination chemistry.
