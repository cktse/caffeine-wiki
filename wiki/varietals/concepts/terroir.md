# Terroir — Environmental Origin Factors

> This page covers Layer 1 and 2 of the coffee terroir model: environmental origin factors and variety. For the full cross-domain terroir framework — including post-harvest processing as terroir, the assessment protocol, and cross-domain links — see [[shared/terroir]].

## Definition

The combined environmental conditions — altitude, soil composition, rainfall, temperature variation, and microclimate — that shape a coffee's flavor potential at the farm. Williams et al. (2022) propose the formal definition: "The terroir of coffee is defined as the unique sensory experience derived from a single origin roasted coffee that embodies its source." In specialty coffee, terroir explains why the same variety grown in different locations tastes different in the cup.

## Why It Matters

Terroir is the justification for single-origin coffee as a category and for the price premium that specialty markets pay for provenance. Understanding which terroir factors drive which sensory outcomes informs green bean sourcing decisions: when you know that altitude drives density, diurnal range drives sweetness, and soil drives mineral complexity, you can predict which origins will express specific cup qualities.

## Key Factors

- **Altitude** — higher elevation = cooler temperatures = slower cherry maturation = denser beans = higher acid potential. Coffee from >1,200m is classified Strictly Hard Bean (SHB) and associated with fruity, floral, complex cup profiles. All specialty Panama Geisha is from 1,300–2,000m. Altitude >1,000m with rainfall <1,600mm: aromatic, slightly bitter, acidic, good body. Altitude <850m with rainfall >2,110mm: bitter, grassy, lower aroma, astringent. (Williams 2022)
- **Temperature sensitivity** — even a 2.5°C temperature difference between sites can produce a significant shift in sensory score, enough to change quality grade. Microclimates matter at a scale that coarse altitude data cannot capture. (Williams 2022)
- **Diurnal temperature range** — large day/night swings slow sugar metabolism; cherries accumulate more sucrose over a longer maturation period, which translates to enhanced sweetness and complexity in the cup
- **Soil** — volcanic soils (Ethiopia, Guatemala, Panama's Volcán Barú) and drainage characteristics are frequently cited by producers and the specialty trade. However, the peer-reviewed evidence is **inconclusive** (Williams 2022): soil type co-varies with altitude, climate, and variety in virtually all field settings, making controlled isolation of soil's direct effect nearly impossible. Plausible mechanisms exist (K/Ca/Mg → enzyme function and acid synthesis in the cherry; drainage → root oxygenation → maturation quality), but their effect size in the cup is unknown. Commercial claims about soil likely overstate certainty. See [[shared/terroir]] for the full evidence critique.
- **Microclimate** — Pacific vs. Caribbean slope exposures, rain shadow effects (e.g., Panama's Volcán Barú creates distinct microclimates on its eastern and western faces), proximity to cloud forest cover
- **Shade / agroforestry** — effect is altitude-dependent; at low altitude, shading extends maturation by cooling → positive for quality; at high altitude, further shade reduction of radiation at an already cool/low-radiation site may reduce quality. Effect is modified by variety and tree age. See [[entities/ethiopia]] for Ethiopian agroforestry context. (Williams 2022)

## Terroir vs. Processing vs. Roasting

A key framing from Ledezma et al. (2025): "Roasting is not considered a factor that drives terroir, because flavor and aroma precursors are already present within the green coffee beans. However, the roasting process is critical to terroir as the roasting profile can **enhance or diminish** the presence of those precursors and the resulting aromas and flavors."

The practical implication:
1. **Terroir is set at the farm** (altitude, soil, microclimate, variety)
2. **Processing expresses it** — washed processing is more terroir-transparent; natural processing adds a fermentation layer that can partially mask or amplify terroir characteristics
3. **Roasting reveals or suppresses it** — light-medium roast is recommended for terroir-transparent cups (Ledezma et al.); darker roast overlays roasting-derived compounds (Maillard/caramelization) that can eclipse origin character
4. **Brewing method affects expression** — different brewing methods (immersion vs. percolation) alter which volatile compounds are extracted; the same terroir can express differently through V60 vs. French press

## Empirical Evidence in This Wiki

### Panama Geisha — Production Zone Differentiation (Ledezma et al. 2025)

The first peer-reviewed terroir study for Panama Geisha, comparing four western Panama production zones from the 2023-2024 harvest. All samples washed, all SHB. Key finding: **sensory differences exist and are statistically confirmed; macro physicochemical parameters converge across zones**.

- Discriminant analysis confirmed differences among Boquete, Tierras Altas (Volcán), and Renacimiento at 95% confidence
- Physicochemical parameters (roast degree, density, pH, color) did NOT differ significantly — terroir is encoded in volatile compounds
- Bergamot fingerprint present across ALL zones — a variety constant, not a regional differentiator

See [[concepts/geisha]] for the full regional sensory profile table.

**Methodological note**: Study used a consumer panel (RATA, n=24), not Q-graders or SCA cupping protocol. Single harvest year. The findings are directionally valid but should be treated as initial evidence, not definitive terroir definition.

## Open Questions

- Can terroir be disentangled from varietal genetics in blind tasting? Ledezma et al. study controlled for variety (all Geisha) and processing (all washed) — making it a cleaner terroir test than most.
- Do the Panama terroir differences (Boquete vs. Tierras Altas vs. Renacimiento) replicate across harvest years? Single-harvest limitation.
- How does altitude interact with soil type to produce the observed sensory differences? Environmental data not collected in the Ledezma et al. study. The confounding problem makes this the most understudied Layer 1 factor — see [[shared/terroir]] (Soil — The Evidence Problem).
- In Ethiopian production, how much of the cup character from a specific region (Yirgacheffe, Guji, Sidama) is terroir vs. landrace variety vs. processing method? Most Ethiopian specialty is washed or natural — controlling for one variable would isolate the others.
- Does Boquete's "fruity/bitter" profile reflect zone-level terroir or farm-level variation? The 11-sample Boquete group includes significant within-zone altitude variation (1,450–2,000m).
