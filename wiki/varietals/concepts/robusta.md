# Robusta (*Coffea canephora*)

## Definition

*Coffea canephora* — commercially known as robusta — is one of two globally cultivated coffee species (131 total Coffea species exist). Wild origins: central and western sub-Saharan Africa, from Guinea to Uganda to Angola, in humid understorey forests at 50–1500m elevation. Cultivation range is lower and hotter than arabica: 200–800m ASL.

Regional names: "robusta" (global commodity term), "conilon" (Brazil, from "koilou"), "nganda" (Central Africa), "koilou/quillou" (French colonial designation).

## Why It Matters (in this domain)

Robusta now accounts for ~40% of global coffee production (up from 25% in the early 1990s) and is closely genetically related to arabica — CLR resistance in all arabica breeding programs traces back to a wild robusta–arabica cross (see [[concepts/timor-hybrid]]). Its genetic diversity pool remains largely unexplored and may be essential for arabica resilience against climate change and disease.

## Key Biological Characteristics

**Caffeine / chemistry**: Higher caffeine, lower sugar, higher soluble solids than arabica.

**Cup profile**: Lower acidity, higher bitterness, "full-bodied." Pyrazine content → earthy aroma. With proper processing and fermentation, specialty-grade robusta is possible (Uganda Coffee Development Authority, 2019).

**Critical agronomic fact — Allogamy**: Robusta is **allogamous** and **cannot self-pollinate**. It requires pollen from a genetically different plant. Arabica is autogamous (can self-fertilize), so this is a fundamental biological difference.

Practical implications:
- Robusta plantations are **never genetically uniform** — always a mix of types
- Farmers must plant multiple compatible clone types for successful pollination
- Cross-compatibility is genetically controlled; simultaneous flowering is required
- Two regional approaches: (1) West Africa = polyclonal seed varieties (mixed types in same packet); (2) Brazil = tested compatible clones released as paired clonal seedlings

## Two Genetic Groups

| Group | Wild Origin | Agronomic Traits | Cup |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Guinean** | Central-west Africa (Ivory Coast, Guinea, Gabon) | Narrow internodes, secondary branching, early harvest, **drought resistant** | High caffeine, **low bean weight** |
| **Congolese** | Central Africa (DRC, Uganda) | Tall, large internodes, late harvest, **drought susceptible** | Higher rust resistance, **high bean weight**; most widespread globally |

Most commercially grown robusta is Congolese-type or Congolese-derived. The Guinean gene pool is smaller and under threat from genetic erosion as Congolese-type trees are introduced into its native range (Ivory Coast).

## Dispersal History

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| ~1870 | First documented cultivation, Congo (Lomani River region) |
| 1880 | "Koilou" subtype observed by French, Gabon/Congo River mouth |
| 1895 | Named *C. canephora* by botanist Louis Pierre |
| ~1900 | Belgian Congo seeds → Brussels → Java, Indonesia (fastest adoption: CLR-devastated arabica farms switch to robusta) |
| 1900–1910 | Ivory Coast, Guinea, Uganda introductions |
| 1912 | Conilon group introduced to Brazil (Espírito Santo) |
| 1916 | INEAC (Belgian Congo) established; major selection center 1930–1960 |
| 1930–35 | Reaches Central America via Guatemala |

The CLR outbreak of the late 1800s was the primary driver of global robusta adoption — arabica collapsed, robusta was resistant.

## Climate Resilience — A Nuanced Picture

Robusta is commonly cited as more climate-resilient than arabica. WCR (2023) cautions this is **overstated**:

- Temperatures above **20.5°C** significantly reduce yields
- High precipitation requirements remain
- Multiple disease/pest threats: coffee wilt disease, red blister disease, stem borer, CBD, CBB, nematodes — and even CLR (robusta has natural resistance but is not immune)

As arabica cultivation zones shift under climate change, robusta may expand its role. But it is not a simple heat-tolerant substitute.
