# Yemen

## What / Who

The primary domestication center of *Coffea arabica*. Coffee seeds were introduced from neighboring Ethiopia (likely from the Hararghe/Eastern Rift area) and cultivated in Yemen from approximately the 15th century. Virtually all arabica varieties cultivated outside Ethiopia transited through Yemen before dispersal to the world. The Mocha port (Al-Mukha, on the Red Sea) was the global hub for coffee trade from the 15th through early 18th century — so central that "mocha" became synonymous with coffee itself.

## Relevance

Yemen is the missing link in arabica dispersal history. Ethiopia holds wild genetic diversity; Yemen domesticated it and then seeded the world. Three distinct genetic clusters remain in Yemen today — including a "New-Yemen" cluster not found anywhere else on earth (Montagnon et al. 2021). Yemen's growing conditions (extreme temperature ranges, very low rainfall, high altitude) have produced stress-adapted genetics over 600+ years that are of potential value for climate-resilient breeding.

## Key Facts

- **Domestication role**: Historical records from the 15th century onward confirm coffee cultivation in Yemen before any other country; all arabica cultivated outside Ethiopia transited through Yemen (confirmed by SSR genetics, Montagnon et al. 2021 and prior studies)
- **Three Yemen genetic clusters** (Montagnon et al. 2021):
  - **Yemen Typica-Bourbon cluster**: The main route — includes Bourbon, Typica, SL-28, Kent, Moka varieties; still present in Yemen 300 years after dispersal
  - **Yemen SL-34 cluster**: Distinct from Typica-Bourbon; SL-09 and SL-34 trace to this Yemen subpopulation via French Mission (seeds from Aden)
  - **New-Yemen cluster**: 24 accessions unique to Yemen; not found in any worldwide cultivar or sampled Ethiopian accession; unknown Ethiopian ancestors; 100% accurately classified by discriminant analysis
- **New-Yemen significance**: Either never left Yemen or was lost en route during 18th-century dispersal. Represents a genetic reservoir shaped by extreme Yemen climate stress — potential for drought tolerance, high-temperature adaptation, and unexplored cup quality.
- **Extreme growing conditions**: Yemen has among the widest temperature ranges and lowest rainfall of any arabica-producing country globally; steep terraced farms (750–2,500m)
- **Mocha heritage**: Al-Mukha (Mocha) was the dominant global coffee export port from ~1500 to ~1720; "Mocha" coffee became synonymous with Yemen origin and the port's distinctive cup profile
- **Current production**: Low and declining due to conflict (since 2015), water scarcity, and infrastructure damage; specialty Yemen coffees (Mocha, Ismaili, Jaadi) are available in limited quantities at high prices
- **Farmer variety names**: Local names (Udaini = tall trees; Dawairi = compact; Tufahi = round fruit) are based on visual morphology, not genetic identity — no correlation between farmer names and genetic clusters (Montagnon et al. 2021; Hussein et al. 2017)

## Growing Conditions

- Altitude: typically 1,500–2,500m on mountain terraces
- Rainfall: very low; much of production in semi-arid conditions; supplemental irrigation or fog collection in some areas
- Temperature: extreme diurnal ranges; both high daytime temperatures and cold nights — more extreme than any major specialty origin
- Terraced farms: traditional terrace agriculture on steep Yemeni mountains; labor-intensive; no mechanization possible
- Varietal selection: 600+ years of farmer selection under stress conditions has produced the most climate-adapted arabica genetics in the world

## Cup Profile (Mocha Heritage)

Yemen Mocha/Ismaili cups are distinct from Ethiopian or Latin American profiles:
- Wild, winey, fermented/jammy complexity
- Chocolate, tobacco, and spice notes alongside fruit
- Medium body; distinctive earthiness from natural processing (most Yemen coffee is natural-processed)
- Inconsistency: small farm, natural processing without standardization; lot quality varies widely
