# Does Coffee Have Terroir and How Should It Be Assessed?

**Source**: Williams, S.D., Barkla, B.J., Rose, T.J., Liu, L. 2022. *Foods* 11, no. 13: 1907. MDPI. DOI: 10.3390/foods11131907
**PMC**: PMC9265435
**Published**: 2022-06-27
**Credibility**: **High-Medium** — peer-reviewed, PMC-indexed; cited as the framework paper by subsequent empirical studies including Ledezma et al. 2025 (Panama Geisha); synthesizes a substantial literature base. Limitations: review paper with no new experimental data; MDPI open-access tier; funded by AgriFutures Australia (minor commercial interest in promoting Australian specialty coffee terroir value).

## Summary

A comprehensive review proposing a formal definition and assessment framework for coffee terroir — the first such synthesis in the peer-reviewed literature at the time of publication. Working from cup to farm, the paper identifies what determines terroir, what affects its assessment, and proposes a standardized assessment method. Key structural argument: unlike wine, coffee terroir should include post-harvest processing because the processing method is often environmentally determined. Proposes cupping (SCA standard, medium-light roast, Agtron 65) as the reference method for terroir assessment.

## Key Claims

- **Formal definition**: "The terroir of coffee is defined as the unique sensory experience derived from a single origin roasted coffee that embodies its source"
- **Terroir is fixed at cherry harvest** — roasting, grinding, and brewing affect terroir EXPRESSION, not terroir itself
- **Coffee terroir broadly encompasses**: all factors affecting the growth of the coffee cherry and its conversion to a green coffee bean
- **Post-harvest processing IS part of coffee terroir** (unlike wine): the processing method is often environmentally determined (Colombia's high rainfall forces washed processing; dry regions use natural); therefore environment still shapes the cup through processing
- **Terroir assessment requires standardization** of roast level, grind size, and brew method to observe origin differences
- **Cupping is the best method** for terroir assessment: sits between espresso (too concentrating; favors roasted/bitter) and filter (oil removal; favors fruity) — occupies a middle position that minimizes brew-method bias
- **Roasting**: should be medium-light (Agtron 65, SCA standard); lighter roasts preserve terroir-relevant precursors; darker roasts overlay Maillard-derived compounds that obscure origin character
- **Altitude >1,000m, rainfall <1,600mm**: aromatic, slightly bitter, acidic, body — higher quality
- **Altitude <850m, rainfall >2,110mm**: bitter, grassy, lower aroma, astringent — lower quality
- Even a **2.5°C temperature change** has significant effect on sensory score — enough to change quality grade; implication: microclimates matter
- Higher altitude coffee has more CGA and lipids; more citric/floral/sugar cane notes; less chocolate/almond; higher caffeine but may taste LESS bitter (other compounds dilute bitterness at slow maturation)
- **Variety is the next most important factor after altitude**: Caturra at >800m → fruity/acidic/tarty; Typica at >800m → balanced/bitter/full-bodied
- **Shading is context-dependent**: beneficial at low altitude (extends maturation via cooling); potentially negative at high altitude (further reduces radiation at an already cool/low-radiation site)
- **The brew method can enhance but NOT CHANGE terroir sensory attributes** — it modifies intensity and balance, not the underlying characteristics
- Biochemical markers (GCMS, HPLC-QTOF, NIR, Raman) can identify terroir fingerprints and should be used to validate, not replace, sensory assessment
- The WCR Sensory Lexicon (110 descriptors) provides the vocabulary for standardized terroir description; subsets (36 descriptors from Cup of Excellence) are practical for studies

## Brew Method Comparison for Terroir Assessment

| Method | Terroir expression bias | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cupping (SCA) | Balanced / industry reference | 8.25g/150mL, 93°C, 3-5 min, medium-light roast; minimal equipment |
| Filter / pour-over | Fruity notes emphasized; reduced bitterness | Paper filter removes oils; changes balance |
| Espresso | Bitterness and roasted notes enhanced | Concentration obscures delicate terroir |

## Processing Methods and Terroir

| Method | Sensory contribution | Terroir link |
|---|---|---|
| Washed | Fruity, sweet, floral, caramel, acidity | Chosen where rainfall prevents sun drying; the environment determines the method |
| Honey | Balanced sweetness and body; between washed and natural | Retained mucilage → increased polysaccharides → sweet/body |
| Natural | Red wine notes, strong body; inconsistent batch-to-batch | Chosen for dry/hot regions; drying rate is environmentally dependent |

## Key Entities Mentioned

- SCA (Specialty Coffee Association) — cupping standard (8.25g/150mL, 93°C, Agtron 65, medium-light roast)
- WCR Sensory Lexicon — 110 descriptors; subset of 36 identified from Cup of Excellence literature
- [[entities/colombia]] — example of environment determining processing method (high rainfall forces washed)
- Decazy et al. 2003 — Honduras 6-region cupping study; altitude-quality correlation; Santa Barbara astringent/sour at same roast profile as higher-quality regions

## Contradictions / Open Questions vs. Existing Wiki

- Williams 2022 was cited by Ledezma et al. 2025 (Panama terroir study) — provides the framework that the Panama study was designed to test. Connection strengthens both sources.
- Shade-quality relationship is more nuanced than previously noted in [[entities/ethiopia]]: benefit at low altitude, potential detriment at high altitude. The Ethiopian agroforestry page discusses ecological benefits but does not resolve the quality direction at high altitude.
- The paper notes soil has generally minor direct terroir impact compared to altitude, temperature, and variety — but affects nutrient availability and drainage.
