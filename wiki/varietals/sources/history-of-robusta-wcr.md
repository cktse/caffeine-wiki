# History of Robusta (World Coffee Research)

## Summary

Authoritative history and genetics overview of *Coffea canephora* (robusta) from WCR. Covers wild origins in sub-Saharan Africa, dispersal history from Congo through Java to global cultivation, two genetic groups (Guinean and Congolese), allogamous (cross-pollinating) reproduction requiring mixed-clone farming, cup quality characteristics, and the species' growing role as arabica faces climate change pressure. Key finding: robusta's reputation for climate resilience may be overstated — temperatures above 20.5°C significantly reduce yields.

## Key Claims

- *C. canephora* wild origins: central and western sub-Saharan Africa (Guinea to Uganda to Angola); humid understorey forests, 50–1500m elevation
- One of 131 Coffea species; one of two commercially cultivated at global scale
- Production share: rose from 25% to 40% of global coffee since early 1990s; 95% produced by 6 countries (Vietnam, Brazil, Indonesia, Uganda, India, Côte d'Ivoire)
- Cultivation range: hotter, more humid, lower elevation than arabica (200–800 m ASL)
- Higher caffeine, lower sugar, higher soluble solids, less susceptible to pests/diseases vs. arabica
- **Allogamous: cannot self-pollinate.** Requires pollen from two genetically different plants → plantations are never genetically uniform; farmers must grow multiple clone types
- Two genetic groups: **Guinean** (central-west Africa) and **Congolese** (central Africa; most widespread)
- Cup quality: lower acidity, higher bitterness, "full-bodied" due to pyrazine (earthy aroma compound); can serve specialty markets when properly processed
- Fermentation quality control "can make an immediate and crucial difference in robusta production and quality"
- CLR resistance was the primary reason robusta spread rapidly into Southeast Asia when arabica was devastated by rust in the late 1800s
- Climate resilience may be overstated: temperatures above **20.5°C** significantly reduce yields; high precipitation needs
- Genetic diversity is vast; cultivated robusta has changed relatively little from wild progenitors; much unexplored potential in the gene pool
- Congolese-type introductions into Ivory Coast threaten genetic integrity of wild Guinean gene pool

## Dispersal Timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| ~1870 | First documented cultivation, Congo (Lomani River region) |
| 1880 | "Koilou" subtype observed in wild by French (Gabon/Congo River mouth) |
| 1895 | Named *Coffea canephora* by botanist Louis Pierre |
| 1897 | First formal species description published (Froehner) |
| 1900 | Edouard Luja collects seeds for Paris Exposition; Belgian Congo becomes dispersal center |
| ~1900 | Congo seeds → Brussels → Java (Indonesia); first systematic robusta breeding conducted in Java |
| 1900–1910 | Ivory Coast, Guinea, Uganda introductions |
| 1910+ | Java material reintroduced to Central Africa |
| 1912 | Conilon group introduced to Brazil (Espírito Santo) |
| 1916 | Belgian Congo: INEAC established; center for selection 1930–1960 |
| 1930–35 | Central America via Guatemala |

## Two Genetic Groups

| Group | Origin | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Guinean** | Central-west Africa | Narrow internodes, high caffeine, low bean weight, drought resistant, secondary branching, early harvest |
| **Congolese** | Central Africa | Higher rust resistance, medium caffeine, high bean weight, drought susceptible, larger internodes, tall, late harvest; most widespread globally |

## Allogamy — Farming Implications

Because robusta cannot self-pollinate, mixed planting is required:
- West Africa approach: polyclonal seed varieties (multiple types in same seed packet)
- Brazil approach: compatible clones tested and released as paired clonal seedlings

Cross-compatibility is genetically controlled — not all varieties can fertilize each other. Simultaneous flowering is a key compatibility consideration.
